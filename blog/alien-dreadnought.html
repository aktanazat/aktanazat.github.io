The Alien Dreadnought

November 2026

In 2016 Elon Musk told investors that Tesla's code name for its factory, "the machine that builds the machine," was the alien dreadnought. By April 2018, with Model 3 output short of its goal, he took it back: "Excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake. Humans are underrated."

This year Tesla started early production of its Optimus robot in Fremont. Musk was early. The lights-out factory, a plant that can run in the dark because no one needs to see, is getting closer, and America holds more of it than the robot counts suggest.

America invented the industrial robot: the first Unimate went to work at a General Motors plant in 1961. The arm business later went abroad, to Switzerland's Stäubli and Japan's Omron, and today most robots installed in the United States come from Japan and Europe. But a robot is now more than an arm, and the layer that tells it what to do is where America leads.

Where America leads

Amazon runs more than a million robots across more than 300 sites. Figure raised over $1 billion at a $39 billion valuation in 2025, the year Nvidia released an open AI model for humanoid robots. America builds the models and the software and designs the leading chips, and investors are paying for that lead.

The payoff is proven

No plant is fully lights-out yet, and the partial ones already pay. Xiaomi's Beijing factory makes one phone every six seconds, run by technicians from a control room. In Dongguan, Changying Precision cut a process from 650 workers to 60.

Room to grow

Per worker, American factories are among the most automated anywhere: 323 robots per 10,000 employees, eighth in the world, against China's 193. China wins on volume, with 354,200 robots installed in 2025 to America's 38,400.

The bigger opening is at home. In 2022 only 6.4 percent of American plants had any robot at all. Owners name poor fit and the cost of setup as the barriers, which is what general-purpose robot AI is built to remove.

The parts left to build

America buys its robot arms from Japan and Europe. It made 8 percent of the world's machine tools in 2025, against China's 36, and gets most precision gearboxes from two Japanese firms, Nabtesco and Harmonic Drive Systems.

China shows how fast that can change. Its own makers went from 18 to 52 percent of its home robot market in ten years, and in 2025, by the tracker MIR Databank's count, Estun became the first Chinese brand to outsell every rival there.

What it takes

Suppliers, people and patience. Plants cluster: "You need a thousand rubber gaskets? That's the factory next door," a former Apple executive told the New York Times, so each plant that comes back makes the next one easier. Robots need people too: Amazon's newest warehouse needs 30 percent more maintenance and engineering staff, and American manufacturing could need 3.8 million workers by 2033. And automation pays back over years, which rewards the firms willing to wait while most executives chase smooth quarters.

China is putting about $138 billion over 20 years into robotics and AI. America has no robotics strategy yet, but an October 2026 review of robot imports ordered a report by April 2027. A tariff would tax the machines American plants need. The better move is to help them buy robots now while American suppliers grow.

The machine that builds the machine

America already holds the newer half of the alien dreadnought, and China has shown the other half can be built in ten years. The country that invented the industrial robot is in position to build the machine that builds the machine.
