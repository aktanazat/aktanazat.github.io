The Alien Dreadnought

November 2026

In October 2016 Elon Musk told Tesla's investors what he was building. "Our internal code name for the factory, the machine that builds the machine, is the alien dreadnought," he said, and he expected to reach "alien dreadnought version 1" by the summer of 2018. In April 2018, with Model 3 output at 2,020 cars a week against a goal of 2,500, he wrote: "Excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake. Humans are underrated."

The industrial robot is an American invention. Unimation, a company in Danbury, Connecticut, put the first Unimate to work at a General Motors plant in 1961. Westinghouse bought Unimation in 1983 and sold it to Switzerland's Stäubli in 1988. Adept Technology, the largest American maker of robot arms in the 2000s, was bought by Japan's Omron in 2015. Today no large American company builds industrial robot arms, and the robotics industry's own federation says most robots installed in the United States are imported from Japan and Europe.

A lights-out factory is a plant that can run with the lights off because no one needs to see. The idea matters because it changes where things get made: when labor stops being the main cost, the advantage goes to whoever owns the machines, the parts that go into them, and the people who keep them running. On each of those, the United States is behind.

What a lights-out factory actually looks like

Almost no plant in the world runs lights-out from end to end. The famous cases cover one hall, one line, or one shift.

  • Fanuc, Japan. In 2003 the company said robots in one of its plants near Mount Fuji were building about 50 robots per 24-hour shift and could run unsupervised for up to 30 days. That figure is from 2003, and I found nothing newer from Fanuc.
  • Xiaomi, Beijing. The phone factory that opened in Changping in 2024 makes one phone every six seconds, with capacity of up to 10 million a year. Xiaomi's own figure for its automation rate is 81 percent, and a team of technicians runs it from a control room.
  • Changying Precision, Dongguan. In 2015 a phone-parts plant cut a process that needed 650 workers to 60. Output per worker went from about 8,000 pieces a month to 21,000, and the defect rate fell from over 25 percent to under 5.

Every one of these cases is partial, so the useful question is which country is moving toward the lights-off plant fastest.

The count

In 2025 China installed 354,200 industrial robots, 59 percent of the world's total. The United States installed 38,400. China installed about nine robots for every one America did. Since 2015 China's yearly installations grew about fivefold, while America's have stayed between roughly 31,000 and 40,000 a year since 2016.

Measured per worker, the United States looks better: 323 robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees in 2025, eighth in the world, against China's 193. China ranks lower only because its factory workforce is so large. Its total stock of working robots, about 2 million in 2024, was roughly five times America's.

Inside the United States, robots are concentrated in big plants. In 2022, 6.4 percent of American manufacturing plants had any robots at all. Among plants with five or fewer employees the share was 1.6 percent; among plants with 1,000 or more it was 40.9 percent. Firms name poor fit with their work and the cost of integration as the main barriers.

Who makes the machines that make the machines

A lights-out factory needs robot arms, machine tools to cut metal, and the precision parts inside both. The United States is a buyer of all three.

  • Robot arms. The biggest makers by revenue are ABB, Epson, Fanuc, Kawasaki, Yaskawa and KUKA, all Japanese or European. KUKA has been owned by China's Midea since 2017. The closest thing to an American arm maker is Universal Robots, a Danish company owned by Teradyne.
  • Machine tools. In 1981 the United States was the world's largest producer, with about 20 percent of the market. In 2025 China produced $30.8 billion of machine tools, 36 percent of the world's, and the United States produced $7.0 billion, about 8 percent. America is now the world's largest importer of machine tools.
  • Gearboxes. Two Japanese companies, by their own estimates, make most of the precision gearboxes inside robot joints: Nabtesco about 60 percent of the large ones, Harmonic Drive Systems over half of the small ones. I found no leading American maker.

China set out to fix the same dependency. Made in China 2025 targeted 70 percent domestic share of China's robot market by 2025. It reached 52 percent, up from 18 percent in 2015. According to the industry tracker MIR Databank, a Chinese company, Estun, sold more robots in China in 2025 than any other brand, the first time a Chinese brand has led.

Why it keeps happening

Three causes come up again and again in the evidence.

The first is the supplier network. Gary Pisano and Willy Shih of Harvard Business School argued in 2009 that when manufacturing moves offshore, the "industrial commons" goes with it: the engineers, toolmakers and suppliers that new products depend on. A former Apple executive described the difference to the New York Times in 2012: "You need a thousand rubber gaskets? That's the factory next door."

The second is people. Automation does not remove the need for workers so much as change which workers are needed. Amazon says its newest robotic warehouse needs 30 percent more people in maintenance and engineering roles. Deloitte and the Manufacturing Institute estimate American manufacturing could need 3.8 million workers between 2024 and 2033, and that more than 1.9 million of those jobs could go unfilled.

The third is patience. Automation pays back over years. In a survey of 401 American executives, 78 percent said they would give up economic value to keep earnings smooth, and most said they would pass on a profitable project to hit a short-term target.

Tesla's reversal was not the only American attempt to fall short. In Wisconsin, Foxconn promised up to $10 billion and 13,000 jobs in 2017. By the end of 2024 the state counted $717 million invested and 1,242 jobs.

Policy

China has a plan and money behind it. A 2023 plan from its industry ministry set out to double robot density from 2020 levels by 2025, and a state venture fund launched in 2025 is expected to draw nearly 1 trillion yuan, about $138 billion, over 20 years into robotics, AI and other advanced technology.

The United States has no national robotics strategy. Federal base funding for its network of manufacturing research institutes, which includes a robotics institute, was about $160 million in 2023. In October 2026 a national-security review of robot imports found that they threaten national security, imposed no tariffs, and ordered negotiations, with a report due in April 2027. Since most robots used in American factories are imported, a tariff on them would raise the cost of the very equipment a lights-out factory needs.

Where America is strong

The United States leads in what tells a robot what to do. Amazon has deployed more than a million robots across more than 300 sites, and factory-robot statistics mostly leave them out. Figure, a humanoid-robot startup, raised over $1 billion at a $39 billion valuation in 2025. Nvidia released an open AI model for humanoid robots the same year. Tesla has started early production of its Optimus robot in Fremont.

America builds the AI models and the software and designs the leading chips. It imports most of the robot arms, the gearboxes and the machine tools. A lights-out factory needs both halves, and most of the second half comes from abroad.
