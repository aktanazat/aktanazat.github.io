Building DavisDine

April 2026

DavisDine is an iOS app that shows UC Davis students what's being served at the dining commons, with AI-powered meal recommendations, allergen tracking, and nutrition planning. It's on the App Store. This is how it came together.

The problem

UC Davis has four dining commons. The university publishes menus online, but the experience is bad -- slow page loads, no filtering, no way to plan meals across locations. Students with allergies have to manually scan ingredient lists every day. I wanted something that felt native and fast, where you open the app and immediately see what's good today.

Stack

SwiftUI on the front end, Python FastAPI on the back end, deployed on Google Cloud App Engine. Firestore for persistence. The backend scrapes UC Davis menu data on a cron schedule, classifies items using Claude's API, caches everything, and serves it through a REST API. The iOS app is a standard SwiftUI architecture with local caching via UserDefaults for offline use.

Menu scraping and ingredient analysis

The first real technical problem was getting menu data. UC Davis doesn't have a public API, so the backend scrapes and parses their menu pages on a schedule. Each item needs allergen info extracted, descriptions pulled, and ingredients parsed for things like soy, dairy, eggs, sesame, shellfish. This all runs at scrape time so there's zero per-request overhead -- when a student opens the app, everything is pre-computed and cached.

I added AI-powered cuisine categorization on top of this. At scrape time, menu items get batch-classified into 12 categories (American, Mexican, Asian, Italian, Mediterranean, etc.) using Claude Sonnet. 150 items per API call, with a fallback to "other" for anything ambiguous. This lets students filter by cuisine type without any latency cost at request time.

SMS verification

The app has anonymous reviews -- students can rate and review dishes without revealing their identity. But anonymous systems need some form of verification to prevent spam. I implemented SMS verification: you prove you have a real phone number, get a random anonymous handle, and that's your identity in the review system. No names, no bios, no profile photos. Just a phone number hash the server never displays.

Billing spike and cost optimization

About a month in, I noticed a billing spike on Google Cloud. Dug into the usage data and found three culprits: the analytics dashboard was polling too aggressively, rapid deployments during dev sessions were causing expensive cold starts, and the instance size was larger than needed.

Three changes cut costs significantly. Scale-to-zero configuration (min_instances: 0) saved roughly $115/month. Cron frequency went from every 15 minutes to every hour -- 75% fewer API calls with no user-facing difference. Instance downgrade from F2 to F1 halved the per-hour cost. The whole investigation and fix took an afternoon.

User testing and iteration

I ran three rounds of user testing with other students. The feedback was specific and useful. Adding menu items to a meal plan took too many taps -- so I added a quick-add button overlay. The dining commons picker wasn't ordered by proximity or preference -- so I made it sort dynamically based on user rankings. Too much white space on the home screen, not enough color variety in the theme, no undo for accidentally dismissing a recommendation. Each piece of feedback became a GitHub issue, got its own branch, and merged separately.

In one sprint session, I shipped 12 features in about two hours: a favorites screen, station filtering, sort options (by rating, calories, protein), skeleton loaders to replace spinner states, a trending endpoint showing the most-liked items from the past week, review helpfulness voting, a daily meal plan generator, batch ingredient analysis, persistent recommendation caching, and real-time macro totals. Each feature got its own branch, commit history, and PR.

What I'd do differently

I over-built the review system. After watching how students actually used the app, I ended up removing the chat feature and most of the review infrastructure entirely. People wanted quick menu info and meal planning, not a social platform. I should have shipped the minimal version first and waited for pull from users before building social features.

The other lesson was about monitoring. The billing spike would have been caught earlier with alerts in place from day one. I set up cost alerts after the fact, but that's backwards -- infrastructure monitoring should be part of the initial deployment, not a reaction to a surprise invoice.

Where it is now

DavisDine is live on the App Store. Students at UC Davis use it to check menus, track nutrition, and find meals that fit their dietary needs. The backend runs on a single App Engine instance that scales to zero when idle. The whole thing costs less than a meal plan per month to operate.
